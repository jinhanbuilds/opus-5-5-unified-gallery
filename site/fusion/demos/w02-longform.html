i

The Lamp

Salt, oil, brass and glass: the four materials of the keeper's day.

Every evening began with the same small ceremony. The keeper climbed the stair with a can of oil in one hand and a folded cloth in the other, and the tower answered each footfall with a note a little higher than the last. At the top the lens waited under its linen hood like a sleeping animal made of glass.

He polished the prisms in slow circles, never with a bare hand. Salt found its way into everything — the hinges, the clock, the pages of the log — but it was not allowed onto the glass.

The light was not his. He only kept it from going out.

When the sun touched the water he lit the wick, wound the weight and stepped back. The lens began to turn, and the headland became a sentence the sea could read.

ii

The Watch

Night was measured in small tasks. Trim the wick at ten. Wind the clockwork at midnight. Write the weather in a hand steady enough for strangers to trust.

Keeper's clock

19:40
  1. 19:40Lamp lit. Wind south-west, light. Sea slight.
  2. 22:00Wick trimmed. One steamer northbound, lights clear.
  3. 00:10Clockwork wound. Fog on the far headland.
  4. 02:30Moon behind cloud. All well.
  5. 04:40First grey in the east. Lamp put out.

By the small hours the dark had weight. He did not feel alone, exactly. Somewhere past the horizon another keeper was climbing another stair, and between them their two lights stitched the coast together.

iii

The Lens

The largest lenses stand taller than the person who cleans them.

The lens is mostly air. Rings of glass, each prism angled a little differently, catch light that would otherwise leak into the sky and fold it into one level beam. Up close it looks less like a tool than a cathedral window someone has taught to turn.

The lights are automatic now. They turn without anyone climbing the stair. But on clear evenings, from the right headland, the beam still arrives on time — and somewhere inside it is the patience of everyone who kept it.

Notes

  1. The stair in this imagined tower is cast iron, and every tread rings when struck. ↩
  2. Each real lighthouse keeps its own pattern of flashes, so a sailor can tell one headland from the next. ↩
  3. Lenses built from concentric rings of prisms were introduced in the 1820s by the French physicist Augustin Fresnel, replacing far heavier solid lenses. ↩
